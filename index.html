About the Workshop

AI-enabled software and AI-assisted engineering require explanations that support justified decisions. Yet a faithful explanation may not enable stakeholder understanding, and improved understanding may not improve an engineering task. These relationships must be specified and validated when explainability is treated as a first-class software quality attribute.

When AI models are incorporated into software systems, how can explainability become an engineering quality attribute that supports verification, understanding, and actionable decisions?

EXQA treats this as an engineering problem organized around three distinct but interacting concerns:

Faithfulness

Does the explanation accurately reflect the system's actual behavior or decision process?

Understanding

Does the explanation enable a stakeholder to construct an accurate mental model, draw correct task-relevant inferences, and recognize limitations?

Engineering usefulness

Does the explanation improve a concrete software engineering task or decision, such as model selection, defect diagnosis, patch review, or deployment, given its effort and cost?

The scope covers both explanations of AI-enabled system behavior and explanations of AI recommendations in AI-assisted engineering, including model selection, code review, testing, debugging, and architectural evolution. We welcome researchers and practitioners in requirements, architecture, testing, empirical software engineering, human factors, and AI engineering. Preliminary studies, industrial experience, replications, and negative findings are explicitly welcome.

Topics of Interest

Contributions may address faithfulness, stakeholder understanding, engineering usefulness, or their relationships within a clearly identified software engineering context. Topics include, but are not limited to:

  • Explainability requirements and quality models: stakeholder needs, explanation requirements, quality-attribute scenarios, acceptance criteria, and relationships with other software quality attributes.
  • Architectures for explainable software systems: architectural patterns, explanation services and interfaces, evidence provenance, compositional explanations, and system-level explainability across models, services, and agents.
  • Explanation methods, representations, and tools: explanation generation, abstraction, summarization, visualization, interactive explanations, and integration of explanatory support into development and operational environments.
  • Measurement, verification, and testing: faithfulness, validity of explanation metrics, uncertainty, robustness, consistency, explanation testing, and evaluation protocols connecting technical measures to stakeholder and engineering outcomes.
  • Stakeholder understanding and human interaction: mental models, expertise, cognitive load, communication of assumptions and limitations, stakeholder-specific explanations, and human–AI collaboration.
  • Explainability for AI-assisted engineering tasks: requirements analysis, code generation and review, test generation, debugging, vulnerability analysis, architecture decisions, maintenance, and explanations of agent actions and recommendations.
  • Engineering decisions and quality trade-offs: explanation-supported model selection, configuration, integration, and deployment; multi-objective decision support; and trade-offs involving accuracy, reliability, latency, cost, privacy, and security.
  • Explainability throughout software evolution and operation: monitoring, explanation drift, regression testing, versioning, evidence retention, revalidation, runtime adaptation, and governance of explanatory artifacts and responsibilities.
  • Empirical studies and shared research resources: industrial experience, developer studies, longitudinal evaluations, benchmarks, datasets, reusable engineering scenarios, replication, and negative results.

Contributions need not address all three concerns. Each submission should identify the explanatory object, the engineering task, the evidence offered, and any untested assumptions.

How to submit

  • Submissions must use the ACM Primary Article Template (sigconf format), consistent with FSE 2027 requirements.
  • EXQA uses a double-anonymous review process. Remove author names and affiliations, and refer to your own prior work in the third person.
  • Submissions of five pages or fewer (including references) are published as extended abstracts and are not subject to an article processing charge (APC).
  • Submission site: TBA

Review and selection

Each submission will receive at least three reviews from conflict-free program committee members, followed by committee discussion. Selection considers relevance, clarity, originality, soundness, and potential to stimulate discussion, with expectations calibrated to the paper type.

Conflicts of interest

Organizers and their directly supervised students, postdocs, and employees may not submit to EXQA. The workshop follows the ACM SIGSOFT Policy on Program Committees. Conflicted reviewers do not review or take part in decisions on the affected paper; an unconflicted organizer or designated handling chair manages such submissions. Non-organizer PC members may submit under the same standards, with independent handling.

Workshop Format

EXQA is a half-day, in-person workshop. At least half of the programmed time is devoted to discussion and collaborative work. The program includes:

  1. Opening, invited talks, and paper presentations. Two invited talks, one from academia and one from industry, followed by thematic paper sessions. Presentations should state an engineering problem, the contribution, and an open question for discussion.
  2. Thematic working sessions. Small groups organized around requirements, architecture, measurement, human interaction, or lifecycle management each develop a research question, engineering scenario, or evaluation approach.
  3. Panel and collective synthesis. Groups compare findings, discuss competing perspectives, and identify research priorities.

Authors of accepted papers will be invited to provide a short discussion question or scenario before the workshop. Intended outputs are a set of research questions, reusable engineering scenarios, and methodological recommendations.

Attendance is open to all registered FSE participants, including those without an accepted paper. Students and early-career researchers are especially encouraged to join.
